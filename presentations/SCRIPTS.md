### Many small eyes  ·  `idea`  ·  45 s · 109 words

Audio file: `audio/science-7min/idea.wav`

The world's great telescopes are extraordinary, and almost impossible to get time on. Most proposals are turned down, and at any moment most of the sky is not being watched at all.

FACET takes the opposite approach. Instead of one large mirror, a thousand small ones in a shell around the planet, each able to point anywhere it can see. They hand work to one another over optical links, so the fleet behaves as a single instrument. And because there are so many of them, a share of their time is set aside for the public: anyone can ask FACET to look at something, and get the picture back.

### How it works  ·  `how`  ·  60 s · 114 words

Audio file: `audio/science-7min/how.wav`

Here is what that looks like. A member of the public asks to see the Orion Nebula, with ten telescopes for five minutes each. The scheduler finds satellites that can see it and are least busy. Each one turns, a hundred-and-fifty-kilogram spacecraft swinging on its reaction wheels, then settles, finds its guide stars and locks on. Within seconds the request is being observed, and the stacked image comes back with a full account of how it was made.

FACET can also stare. Here a rotating group of telescopes keeps Jupiter under continuous watch. As each satellite sinks toward the Earth's limb, the next has already taken over, so the target is never left alone.

### Planetary defence  ·  `defence`  ·  90 s · 177 words

Audio file: `audio/science-7min/defence.wav`

Now, the reason this matters beyond astronomy. This is an exercise: a fifty-metre asteroid, about the size of the one that flattened two thousand square kilometres of Siberian forest in 1908, on a collision course with the Earth. It is coming from the direction of the Sun.

Telescopes on the ground cannot see it. They look at night, away from the Sun, so it approaches through their blind spot. FACET can look much closer to the Sun, and part of the fleet is always searching.

As the asteroid brightens, a searching telescope catches it, and the fleet confirms the detection by itself within minutes, passing it to neighbouring satellites. Thirty telescopes lock on and follow it, measuring its position every minute. The orbit tightens, and the impact probability climbs from a possibility to a certainty, with hours to spare. In this scenario, the ground network never sees it at all.

Hours of warning are not enough to deflect an asteroid. They are enough to know where it will fall, and to move people out of the way.

### Built to last  ·  `resilience`  ·  45 s · 94 words

Audio file: `audio/science-7min/resilience.wav`

A fleet of small satellites fails gracefully. Here, thirty percent of FACET is switched off at once. Telescope-hours fall with it, but completed observations fall by much less, because the scheduler drops the least urgent work first, and alerts and planetary defence carry on. Replacements are small and cheap, and go up on the next launch.

FACET also shares the sky responsibly: it pauses for the few seconds another satellite crosses its view, screens every satellite for close approaches, and can relay its data through optical links instead of waiting for a ground station.

At the same time, governments now treat asteroid warning and awareness of what is in orbit as priorities. FACET answers both with one piece of infrastructure: a fleet that observes around the clock, can be pointed anywhere in seconds, and grows in capacity with every launch.

### The product  ·  `product`  ·  60 s · 116 words

Audio file: `audio/investor-7min/product.wav`

Using it is simple. A user asks for a target, here the Orion Nebula, with ten telescopes for five minutes each. That is the core product: telescope time on demand, delivered through a web interface and an API.

A second product is continuous monitoring. Here a rotating group of telescopes keeps Jupiter under watch without a break, handing over from one satellite to the next as they pass behind the Earth. The unit we would sell is time on the sky, not hardware.

The architecture is robust by design. There is no single point of failure: here, thirty percent of the fleet is switched off at once, and the system keeps working, shedding its least urgent tasks first. Satellites are replaced on the next launch, not repaired.

Operations scale with software rather than people: scheduling, collision screening, streak avoidance around other constellations and data relay are all automated, and the cost of operating the fleet grows far more slowly than the fleet itself.
